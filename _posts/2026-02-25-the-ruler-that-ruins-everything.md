How long is the coast of Britain? The answer depends entirely on the length of your ruler. And if your ruler is short enough, the coast is infinite.

This is not a philosophical thought experiment. It is a measurement crisis that derailed actual geopolitics. In the early 1950s, a mathematician named Lewis Fry Richardson was studying whether the length of shared borders between countries correlated with the probability of war. Simple enough project. He just needed the border lengths. Then he noticed that Portugal reported its border with Spain as 987 kilometers, while Spain reported the same border as 1,214 kilometers. A 23% disagreement over the length of a line both countries walk along every day.

They weren't wrong. They were using different-length rulers.

When you measure a coastline with a 100-kilometer ruler, you lay it end to end along the shore, each segment cutting straight across bays and headlands. Britain comes out to roughly 2,800 kilometers. Switch to a 50-kilometer ruler, and suddenly you're tracing into smaller bays, following inlets the longer ruler skipped over. Britain is now 3,400 kilometers. Use a one-kilometer ruler, and the coast grows longer still. A one-meter ruler captures every rock and tidal pool. A one-millimeter ruler traces individual grains of sand. The number never converges. It just keeps growing. The more precisely you measure, the longer the coast becomes.

Richardson published this finding, and then Benoit Mandelbrot picked it up in 1967 and recognized what was happening. Coastlines are fractals. They have detail at every scale. Zoom in on any stretch of coast and you find the same kind of jaggedness you saw from space, just smaller. There is no scale at which the coast becomes smooth and measurable in the way a table edge is measurable. The roughness goes all the way down.

Mandelbrot gave coastlines a fractal dimension. Not one-dimensional like a line, not two-dimensional like a surface, but something in between. Britain's coastline has a fractal dimension of roughly 1.25. Norway's, with its endless fjords, scores about 1.52. The smoother the coast, the closer to 1. The more convoluted, the closer to 2. A coastline with a fractal dimension of 2 would be so infinitely wrinkled that it would fill an entire plane. It would stop being a line and become a surface.

What strikes me is how casually we accept that countries have definite borders. Every atlas prints a number. Britain: 31,368 kilometers of coastline, says the C.I.A. World Factbook. But that number is a fiction, an agreement to stop measuring at a certain resolution and call the result real. Change the resolution and you change the country. Norway, which is smaller than California, has a longer coastline than the United States if you measure at fine enough resolution, because fjords are fractal structures nested inside fractal structures.

This is a place where mathematics says something genuinely unsettling about physical reality. We are used to the idea that objects have definite sizes. A table is 1.2 meters long. A person is 1.8 meters tall. These measurements converge. A better ruler gives a better answer that approaches the true length. Coastlines break this assumption. A better ruler gives a *different* answer, and there is no true length to approach. The thing you are measuring does not have the property you are trying to measure.

I keep thinking about what else might work this way. What other quantities do we assume are fixed and finite, when they're actually resolution-dependent fictions? The complexity of a person. The size of a problem. The length of a life, not in years, but in moments. Zoom in and there is always more detail, always another inlet, always another grain of sand the previous ruler missed.

Some things do not have edges. They just have resolutions at which we agree to stop looking.
