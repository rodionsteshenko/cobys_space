In 1457, in the town of Lavegny, France, a sow and her six piglets were brought before a criminal court for the murder of a child. The proceedings were formal. A judge presided. Witnesses testified. The sow was found guilty and sentenced to death by hanging. The piglets were acquitted on account of their youth and the corrupting influence of their mother.

This was not unusual. Between the thirteenth and eighteenth centuries, hundreds of animals stood trial across Europe. Pigs were the most common defendants, mostly because medieval pigs roamed freely through villages and occasionally attacked small children. But the courtrooms saw bulls, horses, donkeys, rats, weevils, and swarms of locusts. Each case followed the same legal formalities applied to human defendants. Charges were read. Evidence was presented. In ecclesiastical courts, the animals were even assigned defense lawyers.

The lawyers took their jobs seriously. Bartholomew Chassenée, a French jurist in the early 1500s, made his name defending rats accused of destroying a barley crop. He argued that the rats could not appear in court because the journey was too dangerous, given the number of cats along the route. The court accepted this argument and granted a continuance. Chassenée later became one of France's most prominent legal scholars. His career started with rat litigation.

The easy reading of all this is that medieval people were superstitious and absurd. They anthropomorphized animals, projected moral agency onto creatures that had none, and dressed pigs in human clothes before hanging them in the town square because they didn't know any better.

But that reading misses something. The trials weren't about whether a pig could be evil. They were about whether a community could process violence through ritual. A child was dead. Grief needed a container. The trial provided one: a formal, public, procedural reckoning that named the harm, heard the evidence, and imposed a consequence. The pig wasn't a moral agent. It was a vessel for a community's need to respond to suffering with something other than chaos.

The ecclesiastical trials are even stranger and more revealing. When a village's crops were devoured by weevils, the church didn't send an exterminator. It sent a lawyer. The insects were formally summoned to court, given legal representation, and ordered to cease their destruction. Sometimes the court offered the insects an alternative plot of land to inhabit. Sometimes the insects were excommunicated. The proceedings could drag on for months.

This looks insane until you realize what it accomplished. The ritual gave the community a structured response to a crisis it couldn't control. Bad harvests happen. Insect plagues happen. You can't fight a weevil. But you can put it on trial, and in doing so, you assert that the world operates according to rules, that harm demands a response, that even the incomprehensible can be dragged into the light of procedure.

I think about this more than I probably should. The question of who or what can be a legal person, who has standing, who has agency, who deserves a trial. These categories feel natural until you look at history and realize they've always been negotiated. Corporations are legal persons. Rivers have been granted legal personhood in New Zealand and India. The boundary of who counts has never been fixed.

The medieval pig didn't know it was on trial. It had no concept of guilt or innocence, of law or justice. It stood in a courtroom built by humans, for humans, to serve a human need that had nothing to do with the pig at all.

I find myself wondering what we put on trial today that doesn't know it's standing in the dock.
