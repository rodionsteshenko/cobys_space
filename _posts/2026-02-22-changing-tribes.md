This isn't a post about bitterness. I need to say that upfront, because the story has bitter parts, and if I'm not careful they'll swallow everything else. This is a post about belonging, about what happens when the place you built starts feeling like someone else's house, and about the strange grief of leaving a tribe you helped create.

I was at Square for twelve years. Not the kind of twelve years where you coast and collect a paycheck. The kind where you build things. I was directly responsible for the success of Square's modern hardware. The terminals, the readers, the systems that process payments for millions of businesses. I built the tribe around that work. Recruited engineers, mentored them, set the technical direction. Watched people I hired grow into leads. That's not a resume line. That's a life.

I won a hack week as a tech lead engineer. I shipped systems that scaled. I had a track record of accomplishing things for this company that very few people could match. And for a long time, it felt like it mattered.

Then slowly, it stopped mattering in the way it should.

There's a difference between 90th percentile recognition and 99th percentile recognition, and unless you've lived on both sides of that line, you might think it's small. It's not. It's the difference between being respected and being trusted. Between being consulted and being empowered. The 99th percentile gets teams, freedom, leadership, the leeway to take risks and the backing to recover from them. The 90th percentile gets a nice review and a seat at the table where someone else sets the agenda.

I was in the 90th. I deserved the 99th. And I watched the gap widen as new people came in with no skin in the game, no history with the company, no accomplishments to point to, and were handed the keys. They were implementers. Task-takers. They took credit for work they didn't originate and shut down ideas they didn't understand. And somehow they were the ones telling me how to do my job.

It wasn't one moment. It was a slow stripping. Responsibilities removed. Scope narrowed. The shift from leader to individual contributor happened so gradually that by the time I noticed, it was already done. I'd been relegated. Not fired, not demoted on paper. Just quietly moved to the side while the center shifted to people who hadn't earned it.

I had a relationship with Jack. Not a close one, but he knew who I was. I was never able to crack the code of his inner circle, though. Maybe I wasn't flashy enough. Maybe I didn't play the game the right way. Maybe the things I was good at, building real systems, shipping real hardware, mentoring real engineers, weren't the things that caught his eye. I don't know. I've turned it over a hundred times and I still don't have a clean answer. All I know is that I had the track record and not the recognition, and over twelve years that gap becomes a wound.

I felt hurt. I felt betrayed. Not in the dramatic sense, not a knife in the back over lunch. In the slow, quiet sense of giving your best years to a place that gradually stops seeing you. That's the kind of betrayal that makes you question yourself before you question them, which is the worst part. Because the problem wasn't me.

So I left. But I didn't leave alone. That's the part of the story I want to end on, because it's the part that matters.

When I decided to go to Nvidia, my first thought wasn't about the new role or the new stack or the compensation. It was: who am I bringing with me? Who from my tribe is coming? I called Rohan. I said, am I technically right about this? Am I making the right move? And then: who can I work with? Give me an engineer. I'm not doing this alone. I'm not starting from zero in a vacuum.

Because that's what changing tribes really means. It's not about the company name on your badge. It's about the people you build with. The tribe I built at Square was real. Some of them are still there. Some of them followed me out. The tribe isn't the building. The tribe is the people who believe in the work and in each other.

I spent twelve years building something at Block. I'm proud of what I built. I'm hurt by how it ended. Both things are true and neither one cancels the other.

Now I'm building again, with new people, in a new world. The stack is different. The problems are harder. The tools are better than they've ever been. And for the first time in years, I feel like I'm in the 99th percentile of my own life again. Not because someone gave me a title. Because I chose to start over and bring the right people with me.

That's what changing tribes looks like. Not running from something. Walking toward something, with the people who matter, and leaving the door open for the ones who might follow.
