In [the last post]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/02/22/engineering-got-isekaid/), I wrote about engineering getting isekai'd. Same tools, new world, everyone's an alchemist now. But I skipped the most important part: what the transmutation actually looks like in practice.

It's not a single incantation. It's a loop.

Here's what really happened. A senior engineer with thirty years of experience switches companies. New stack. C++, Python, GPU infrastructure, Linux networking, virtual machines, complex network topologies. Foreign territory. Stuff he's never touched. In the old world, this would be months of ramp-up. Reading docs, pair programming, slowly building context.

Instead, he sat down with Claude and started willing things into existence.

Not blindly. That's the part people miss. He knew the thing he wanted to build could exist. He'd been in software long enough to know what's possible, even when he didn't know the specific tools. So the conversation went like this: build this. Claude builds something. That's not right. Try this instead. Still not right. What about this approach? Getting closer. What does this network call actually return? Oh, that changes things. Start over from here.

That's the transmutation loop. It's not one spell. It's a sustained act of will. You keep casting until reality bends.

And the key is never letting it stop. Most people try once, get a wrong answer, and conclude the AI can't do it. That's like drawing one transmutation circle, watching it fizzle, and deciding alchemy doesn't work. The alchemist who succeeds draws another circle. And another. And another. Each one informed by what the last one got wrong.

You can literally say to the AI: this thing can exist. I know it can. Why haven't you made it yet? Keep trying. Try a different approach. Start from the beginning. What haven't we considered?

It sounds absurd. It sounds like you're arguing with a compiler. But it works, because the solution space narrows with every iteration. Every failed attempt eliminates a path. Every wrong answer teaches you something about the shape of the right one. You're not guessing. You're converging.

This is where the thirty years matters most. Not in knowing the syntax. Not in typing speed. In knowing what questions to ask. Did you test this? What about this edge case? Does this actually handle the failure mode? A junior dev accepts the first output. A veteran knows what wrong looks like before they even run the code. They can smell it.

The transmutation loop is really a conversation with yourself. The AI is the circle, but you're the alchemist. You're seeing what's available, choosing different paths, exploring the space. It's less like commanding a tool and more like navigating a landscape. You try a direction. Dead end. You backtrack. Try another. Something promising opens up. You push further. Eventually you find the path that works, and it feels obvious in retrospect, the way every solved problem does.

This isn't about engineering anymore. It's about power. Not in the domination sense. In the physics sense. The capacity to do work. To see an opportunity and seize it. To look at an unfamiliar system and say, I don't know how this works yet, but I know the thing I want can exist, and I will not stop until it does.

The equivalent exchange for this power isn't knowledge or autonomy. It's persistence. The willingness to keep drawing circles when the first ten didn't work. Most people give up. The ones who don't are the ones reshaping the world.

That's the real spell. Not the incantation. The refusal to stop casting.
