There's a Slack workspace that exists outside any company. You can't find it from the careers page. You don't get added when you join. You get added when you leave.

It's the Square mafia — what Block alumni call the collection of engineers, PMs, designers, and writers who spent years building one of the more interesting companies in tech and then, one way or another, stopped. Quit, were laid off, moved on. The workspace is the afterlife. A carbon copy of the building, staffed entirely by the undead.

For the first few months after leaving, it's quiet. You join. You scroll. You recognize names. You feel vaguely like you're reading someone else's mail. Then 2,000 people arrive in a week because of a layoff announcement, and suddenly the channels are moving the way they used to on a Tuesday morning in the office, and you realize you haven't felt that in a while.

What's strange about a community like this is that it shouldn't work. The thing that bound you together — the work, the mission, the specific problem you were all pointed at — is gone. The shared context that made the inside jokes legible, that gave the cultural references their weight, exists only in memory. What you're left with is the people, stripped of the premise.

And it turns out that's enough.

There's a podcast about *How I Met Your Mother* where one of the creators and one of the actors just talk. Not about what happened after. About the nine years they spent in the room, building 208 episodes of television. They bring in writers, directors, guest actors. They sit with the thing they made and examine it slowly, the way you turn over something you found on a beach. Not because there's more to sell. Because the making of it mattered and they don't want to forget.

That's what the Square mafia Slack is. A podcast about the show. A place to turn over what you made together and say: that was real, wasn't it. That was something.

What Square was, at its best, was unusually young and unusually earnest. When Rodion joined, the average employee age was 27. He was 33 and felt old. The company was building financial access for small businesses, for street vendors, for people who had been priced out of the banking system. The mission wasn't decorative. People actually believed it. That's a strange thing to maintain as a company scales, and Square maintained it longer than most.

The last five years weren't perfect. The balance of focus-versus-comfort tipped somewhere, maybe. The thing that makes a great run also sometimes makes its ending. That's fine. Every story that ends cleanly was probably lying about something.

What matters is the story is worth telling. Worth sitting with in a Slack channel at 11pm with people you worked alongside a decade ago, laughing at a screenshot from 2018, filing a nit on a commit that will never be merged.

Some companies produce alumni networks. Some produce families. The difference isn't the quality of the work. It's whether the people believed they were building something together, or just building.

Square was the second kind. The workspace is the proof.
