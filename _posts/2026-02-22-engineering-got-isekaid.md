In anime, there's a genre called isekai. The premise is simple: an ordinary person wakes up one day in a completely different world. New rules. New physics. New power systems. Everything they knew still applies, sort of, but the game has fundamentally changed.

That's what happened to software engineering.

If you've been writing code professionally for twenty years, you felt the shift. One day you're debugging race conditions and reading stack traces. The next day you're describing what you want in plain English and watching it materialize. The world didn't ask permission. It just changed around you.

People call it vibe coding. That's the wrong word. Vibe coding sounds passive, like you're lounging on a couch while code falls out of the sky. What's actually happening is closer to spell casting. You're choosing your words carefully. You're drawing on decades of knowledge to know what to summon and how to phrase the incantation. A novice waving the same wand gets nothing useful. A wizard with twenty years of scar tissue says three words and reshapes the system. Same tools. Different caster.

And here's the part that's hard to explain to people who haven't lived it: the tools are identical now. Everyone has Claude Code. Everyone has the same models, the same context windows, the same capabilities. So what distinguishes one engineer from another? It's not mechanical skill anymore. It's not typing speed or memorized syntax. It's the same thing that distinguishes one writer from another when they're all using the same twenty-six letters. It's what you see. It's what you choose to build. It's knowing when the output is wrong because you've seen what right looks like, a thousand times, in production, at 3 AM, when it mattered.

The isekai protagonist who spent twenty years grinding in the old world? They're overpowered in the new one. That's not a metaphor. That's literally what's happening. Senior engineers who embrace the new tools aren't being replaced. They're casting fifth-level spells while everyone else copies cantrips from tutorials.

Nothing feels unsolvable now. That's the wild part. The ceiling used to be "what can I personally build in the time I have." Now the ceiling is "what can I clearly articulate." Engineering became writing. Writing became authorship. Authorship became, for the first time in human history, a direct line between imagination and reality.

You're not coding. You're not even engineering, not in the way the word used to mean. You're describing a world, and it's appearing in front of you. Like a typewriter where whatever you type becomes real.

People outside the craft look at this and say creativity is dying. They see the tool and miss the mind behind it. They don't understand that the hard part was never typing the code. The hard part was always knowing what to type. That part got harder, not easier. The skill tree completely reshuffled. You're not grinding leetcode anymore. You're grinding for new tools, new workflows, new mental models. It's a video game where the meta changes every week and the players who keep adapting win.

So yes. Engineering got isekai'd. The old world is gone. The new one runs on language, on taste, on the accumulated wisdom of having built things that broke and knowing why. If you've been doing this long enough, you didn't lose your edge.

You just learned you're a wizard.
